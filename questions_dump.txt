ID: CP_5D_010_q001
Difficulty: medium
STEM: A researcher purifies a small extracellular protein that contains two disulfide bonds and is biologically active. To probe its structure, she treats the protein with the reducing agent dithiothreitol (DTT) and the denaturant urea, then removes both reagents under conditions that prevent reoxidation. She finds the protein has lost all activity. When she then allows the sample to stand in an oxygen-containing buffer at neutral pH, only about 1 percent of the molecules regain full activity. Which explanation best accounts for the low recovery of activity after reoxidation?

  A. DTT permanently cleaved the peptide bonds linking the cysteine residues, so the polypeptide backbone could no longer fold.
  B. Random reoxidation of the free thiols produced many incorrectly paired disulfide bonds, and only the native pairing yields the active structure.
  C. Urea hydrolyzed the side chains of the cysteine residues, so few cysteines remained available to form any disulfide bonds.
  D. Oxygen oxidized the cysteine thiols all the way to sulfonic acids, preventing the formation of any disulfide linkages.
CORRECT: B
EXPLANATION: DTT reduces disulfide (cystine) bonds back to free cysteine thiols, and urea unfolds the protein, so after treatment the four cysteines are free and the chain is disordered. With four cysteines there are three possible ways to pair them into two disulfide bonds; only one of these pairings matches the native, active structure. When the protein reoxidizes in air without the folding information that urea has erased, the thiols pair largely at random, so only a small fraction reform the correct (native) pattern and regain activity. This matches the roughly 1 percent recovery and identifies B as correct. A is wrong because disulfide bonds are not peptide bonds; DTT reduces S–S linkages, it does not hydrolyze the amide backbone, so the chain remains intact. C is wrong because urea is a denaturant that disrupts noncovalent interactions; it does not hydrolyze cysteine side chains, and the cysteines remain present and capable of forming disulfides. D is wrong because mild air oxidation at neutral pH favors disulfide (cystine) formation, not over-oxidation to sulfonic acid; if all thiols were destroyed, essentially zero molecules would reactivate, yet about 1 percent do recover, consistent with mispairing rather than thiol destruction.

ID: CP_5D_010_q002
Difficulty: easy
STEM: A peptide with the sequence Ala-Gly-Ser-Val is fully hydrolyzed by boiling in $6\ \text{M HCl}$ for 24 hours. Which type of bond is cleaved in this process, and what is the immediate product?

  A. Amide (peptide) bonds are cleaved, releasing the four free amino acids
  B. Disulfide bonds are cleaved, releasing two shorter peptide fragments
  C. Ester bonds are cleaved, releasing free amino acids and water
  D. Hydrogen bonds are cleaved, releasing the intact unfolded peptide chain
CORRECT: A
EXPLANATION: The covalent bonds linking adjacent residues in a peptide are amide (peptide) bonds, formed between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next. Acid hydrolysis with hot concentrated HCl adds water across each of these amide bonds, cleaving the backbone completely and liberating all the constituent free amino acids—here, alanine, glycine, serine, and valine. A is therefore correct. B is wrong because disulfide bonds form only between cysteine side chains, and none of these four residues contains a thiol, so there are no disulfide bonds to break; moreover disulfide cleavage would not break the backbone into separate amino acids. C is wrong because the backbone linkages are amides, not esters; ester bonds contain a C–O–C(=O) linkage, whereas peptide bonds contain a C–N–C(=O) linkage. D is wrong because hydrogen bonds are weak, noncovalent interactions that stabilize secondary structure; disrupting them would denature the peptide but leave the covalent backbone—and thus the intact chain—unbroken, not hydrolyzed into amino acids.

Difficulty: medium
STEM: A biochemist treats a purified protein with the enzyme glutathione reductase together with reduced glutathione, conditions that supply electrons to break a specific covalent linkage between two amino acid side chains. After this treatment, two free cysteine residues are generated where one cross-link previously existed, and the protein partially unfolds. Which statement best characterizes the chemical change that occurred at this linkage?

  A. A disulfide bond was reduced, converting one cystine cross-link into two cysteine thiol groups
  B. A peptide bond was hydrolyzed, releasing two separate cysteine residues into solution
  C. A thioester bond was reduced, regenerating a free thiol and a carboxylic acid
  D. Two cysteine thiols were oxidized, forming a new cystine cross-link between the chains
CORRECT: A
EXPLANATION: The linkage between two cysteine side chains is a disulfide (S–S) bond, and the residue pair joined by this bond is collectively called cystine. Glutathione/glutathione reductase is a physiological reducing system; supplying electrons reduces the disulfide, cleaving the S–S bond and regenerating two free cysteine thiol ($\text{-SH}$) groups. This loss of a cross-link allows partial unfolding, exactly as described. A is correct. B is wrong because hydrolysis of a peptide bond cleaves the polypeptide backbone (a $\text{C-N}$ amide bond), not a side-chain cross-link, and would fragment the chain rather than simply generate two thiols; reduction, not hydrolysis, is occurring here. C is wrong because the cross-link between two cysteines is a disulfide, not a thioester; thioesters link a thiol to an acyl carbon and are not the side-chain cross-links found in folded proteins like this one. D describes the reverse reaction (oxidation forming a disulfide), which is the opposite of what a reducing system accomplishes—reduction breaks the cross-link, it does not create one.

STEM: A researcher studies whether a buried disulfide bond stabilizes a 90-residue enzyme. She compares the wild-type enzyme (one disulfide) with a mutant in which both cysteines are replaced by serine, abolishing the cross-link. Both proteins are purified to homogeneity. She measures the temperature at which each protein is half-unfolded (the melting temperature, $T_\text{m}$) by circular dichroism. To ensure the comparison reflects only the disulfide, all measurements use identical buffer, protein concentration, and heating rate. Critically, she also runs every sample in the presence of $50\ \text{mM}$ dithiothreitol (DTT) as well as without it. What is the primary purpose of including the DTT condition?

  A. To hydrolyze any contaminating peptide bonds so that only intact, full-length protein contributes to the measured $T_\text{m}$
  B. To confirm that any difference in $T_\text{m}$ between wild-type and mutant is actually caused by the disulfide, by reducing the wild-type cross-link in situ
  C. To denature both proteins fully before heating so that the circular dichroism signal starts from the same unfolded baseline
  D. To prevent the free serine hydroxyl groups in the mutant from forming spurious oxygen-based cross-links during heating
CORRECT: B
EXPLANATION: The experiment attributes any stability difference to the single structural change between the two proteins—the presence or absence of the disulfide bond. But wild-type and mutant differ in primary sequence (Cys vs. Ser), so a $T_\text{m}$ difference could in principle arise from the side-chain substitution itself rather than from the cross-link. DTT is a reducing agent that cleaves disulfide bonds, converting cystine back to two free cysteines without altering the sequence. Reducing the wild-type protein in situ should eliminate the stabilizing cross-link, so reduced wild-type should behave like the disulfide-free mutant. If the $T_\text{m}$ gap disappears upon DTT treatment, the difference is genuinely caused by the disulfide; this is an internal control isolating the variable of interest. This makes B correct.

A is wrong: DTT is a reductant of disulfides, not a hydrolytic agent—it does not cleave peptide bonds, which require strong acid or proteases.

C is wrong: DTT does not denature proteins; chemical denaturation requires agents like urea or guanidinium. The experiment deliberately measures thermal unfolding from the native state, so starting from an unfolded baseline would defeat the purpose of measuring $T_\text{m}$.

D is wrong: serine hydroxyl groups do not form oxidative cross-links analogous to disulfides, and DTT (a thiol reductant) would not be the appropriate reagent to suppress such chemistry. The serine substitution is chosen precisely because its hydroxyl is redox-inert under these conditions.

Under dilute conditions, two separate tripeptide molecules join through their cysteine side chains to form a covalently linked dimer rather than an intramolecular ring. If the only new covalent bonds formed are between cysteine side chains and no other groups react, what is the maximum number of intermolecular disulfide cross-links that can form between the two tripeptide molecules in a single dimer?

  A. One, because a disulfide bond can form only once between any two peptide molecules.
  B. One, because each tripeptide contributes only a single reactive thiol overall.

B is wrong because each tripeptide has two cysteines, not one, so it contributes two reactive thiols. D overcounts: each disulfide consumes two thiols (one from each molecule), so four thiols can form at most two bonds, not four; counting four also incorrectly allows a thiol to participate in more than one disulfide simultaneously, which is impossible.

STEM: A pentapeptide has the sequence Gly-Ala-Phe-Lys-Ser. A researcher subjects a $1\ \text{mmol}$ sample to complete acid hydrolysis. Assuming every peptide bond is cleaved and each released amino acid remains intact, how many moles of water are consumed and how many total moles of free amino acids are produced?

  A. $5\ \text{mmol}$ of water consumed and $5\ \text{mmol}$ of amino acids produced
  B. $5\ \text{mmol}$ of water consumed and $4\ \text{mmol}$ of amino acids produced
  C. $3\ \text{mmol}$ of water consumed and $4\ \text{mmol}$ of amino acids produced
  D. $4\ \text{mmol}$ of water consumed and $5\ \text{mmol}$ of amino acids produced

Cleaving all 4 bonds liberates all 5 residues as free amino acids, giving $5\ \text{mmol}$ of amino acids. This matches D. Choice A overcounts the bonds, assuming 5 peptide bonds for 5 residues (it ignores that the chain has free ends, so there are only $n-1$ internal bonds). Choice B uses the correct water amount but mistakenly reports only 4 free amino acids; cleaving 4 bonds releases 5 separate residues, not 4. Choice C undercounts both the water consumed and the amino acids released and is internally inconsistent with the stoichiometry of hydrolysis.

### CARS CARS_P0001 (Linguistics) ###
When a Tlingit elder pauses before answering a stranger's question, the silence may stretch for what an impatient interviewer reads as discomfort, even refusal. To the elder, that interval is the answer's proper preface—a marker of respect proportional to the weight of what was asked. The disagreement here is not about words but about the meaning carried by their absence, and it is precisely this terrain that the study of conversational silence has claimed for itself.

Basso, working among the Western Apache in the 1970s, advanced the foundational claim: silence is keyed to social uncertainty. Apache speakers, he reported, fell silent when relationships were ambiguous—greeting a returning relative whose character might have changed, courting a near-stranger, sitting with the bereaved. Silence, on this account, was not the empty space between communicative acts but a communicative act in its own right, deployed when the appropriate words could not yet be known. Basso took care to insist that the rule was culturally specific; he did not claim that all peoples grow quiet under uncertainty.

It was later interpreters who blurred that caution. A generation of textbooks recast Basso's situated observation as a near-universal: where relationships are unsettled, the argument ran, humans default to reticence. Tannen, examining American conversational style, complicated this tidy picture. She distinguished speakers oriented toward "high involvement," for whom overlapping talk and rapid turn-taking signal engagement, from those oriented toward "high considerateness," for whom pauses signal respect. A silence that one speaker offers as courtesy, she observed, the other may experience as a rebuff—and then fill, prompting the considerate speaker to conclude that the floor was never truly his. The misreading, crucially, runs in both directions; neither party is simply wrong.

Tannen's intervention reframed silence as relational rather than absolute, but it left Basso's central correlation—uncertainty produces silence—largely intact. That correlation is what a more recent body of work has unsettled. Comparative studies of turn-taking across unrelated languages found that the gap between conversational turns is strikingly short and strikingly uniform, clustering near a fifth of a second regardless of the language. On this evidence, sustained silence is not a default that uncertainty switches on but an effortful departure from a baseline of near-instant response. If so, Basso's Apache were not relaxing into a natural reticence; they were actively suspending a powerful pull toward rapid reply.

This finding does not so much refute Basso as relocate him. The uniform brief gap suggests that what varies across cultures is not whether silence means something—everywhere a held pause is conspicuous against the quick rhythm of exchange—but which social conditions a community treats as warranting that conspicuous departure. Apache uncertainty and Tlingit deference become two answers to a shared question: when is it worth breaking the rhythm?

Yet one attempt to extend the timing studies to ritual and ceremonial speech failed to find the expected uniformity; in those settings, the brief gap dissolved into long, patterned silences that no participant treated as marked. The exception is instructive. It implies that the rapid-response baseline governs ordinary conversation specifically, not speech as such, and that the very contrast on which silence's meaning depends can be suspended by genre. A silence in a courtroom, a sermon, or a lament may signify nothing at all—not because the participants share Basso's rule or violate Tannen's, but because the clock against which silence is read has been switched off.

### CARS CARS_P0002 (Studies of Diverse Cultures) ###
When the Garifuna of the Caribbean coast of Central America perform the dügü, a multi-day ceremony summoning ancestral spirits to heal an afflicted descendant, an observer might reasonably classify it as religion. Yet the anthropologist Byron Foster, working in Belize in the 1980s, resisted that classification. The dügü, he insisted, was less a system of belief than a technology of obligation: its function was to reassert that the living owed the dead a debt payable only in collective labor, food, and presence. To call it religion, Foster argued, was to import a Protestant assumption that the core of ritual lies in interior conviction rather than in the public discharge of duty.

Foster's reframing drew on an older quarrel. Émile Durkheim had located the power of ritual in its capacity to make a society feel its own solidarity; the sacred, for him, was the group worshipping itself in disguised form. A later generation, influenced by Clifford Geertz, found this functionalism too tidy. Geertz proposed that rituals were texts to be read, dramatizations through which a people articulated and revised what they took the world to be like. On this view the dügü was not merely cementing obligation; it was a statement about the porousness of the boundary between the living and the dead, a claim the Garifuna were making to themselves about the nature of personhood.

For a time these two readings coexisted uneasily, the one stressing what ritual does, the other what it says. The ethnographer Paul Christopher Johnson, returning to Garifuna communities decades after Foster, complicated both. His fieldwork tracked Garifuna who had migrated to New York and then returned home to commission ceremonies they could no longer regularly attend. Among these transnational participants, Johnson found, the dügü had acquired a function neither Durkheim nor Geertz anticipated: it served to certify authenticity, to prove that the migrant remained, despite distance and dollars, a genuine Garifuna. The ritual had become, in part, a performance addressed to an imagined external audience of Belizeans, North Americans, and the migrants' own uncertain self-image.

Notably, Johnson revised a position he had earlier defended. In his first writings he had treated such diaspora ceremonies as diluted versions of a once-integral practice, corrupted by the logic of the marketplace. Confronted with participants who spoke movingly of the dügü as the one thing money could not finally buy—since the ancestors, they said, could detect insincerity—he abandoned the language of dilution. The ceremony was not decaying; it was doing new work under new conditions, and the older work had perhaps never been as stable as the salvage-minded ethnographer assumed.

This last point cuts against a temptation common to all three thinkers' admirers: the belief that a ritual has an original, essential meaning from which later forms deviate. If Johnson is right, the search for what the dügü really is mistakes a moving target for a fixed one. A skeptic might object that this verges on saying the ceremony means whatever its practitioners need it to mean, draining the concept of analytic content. Johnson's reply is instructive. The ancestors, he notes, are imagined as exacting auditors who reject hollow gestures; the practitioners themselves police authenticity. The meaning is neither fixed nor infinitely pliable. It is negotiated, under constraints the participants take with deadly seriousness, by people who would be baffled to learn that scholars regard their negotiations as the interesting part.

### CARS CARS_P0003 (Political Science) ###
When citizens of a struggling democracy tolerate a leader who bends the rules, observers often reach for the language of pathology: voters have been duped, or have abandoned their principles. Yet a quieter scholarly debate concerns whether such tolerance might be, in its own terms, rational. The dispute turns on what voters believe they are choosing when they reward a norm-breaker at the ballot box.

The earliest systematic answer came from Pradhan, who argued in the 1990s that support for transgressive leaders reflects a straightforward exchange. Voters, she contended, knowingly trade procedural integrity for tangible delivery—roads, jobs, security—because formal institutions have failed them so repeatedly that the rules themselves seem like a luxury affordable only to the comfortable. On this account, the citizen is not deceived but pragmatic, weighing the cost of broken procedures against the benefit of broken logjams.

Moreno disputed the premise of a clean trade. Drawing on interviews across several Latin American municipalities, he held that voters rarely perceive any sacrifice at all. What scholars label "norm violation," he argued, ordinary partisans recode as decisiveness or loyalty to their side; the very act of bending a rule is read as evidence that the leader will fight for them rather than for an abstract system that has never served them. The voter, in Moreno's telling, is not balancing goods against costs but redefining what counts as a cost in the first place. Where Pradhan saw a calculated exchange, Moreno saw a shifting frame.

Notably, Pradhan herself later complicated her original view. Returning to her data after two decades, she conceded that the "exchange" model assumed voters could readily identify which acts were transgressions—an assumption her newer evidence undercut. She did not adopt Moreno's framing wholesale, however. Voters, she now suggested, often recognized a violation perfectly well when it harmed a rival but declined to recognize the identical act when committed by their own side. This was neither pure pragmatism nor pure reframing, but a selective vision that depended on partisan position. The trade was real, she maintained, but its terms were not applied evenhandedly.

A third strand, associated with the work of Adeyemi, attempted to test these claims experimentally by presenting subjects with descriptions of leaders who violated procedural norms and measuring shifts in approval. The studies produced an awkward result: in several replications, exposure to a co-partisan's violation produced no measurable change in support, neither the penalty Pradhan's exchange model implied nor the boost Moreno's reframing predicted. Adeyemi was careful not to overstate the finding. A null result, he noted, might mean that the behaviors he described were too mild, that respondents distrusted the hypothetical scenarios, or that approval is simply a blunt instrument for capturing a judgment that operates below the level of stated opinion.

What the experimental difficulty exposes is how much each position rests on inference about interior states that resist direct observation. Pradhan reads calculation, Moreno reads reinterpretation, and each can cite the same electoral outcome as confirmation. The behavior—a vote cast, an approval expressed—underdetermines the mental accounting behind it. This is less a failure of any one theory than a reminder that the study of political tolerance must work largely by reconstruction, building plausible accounts of motives that the voters themselves may never articulate, and that no tally of votes can adjudicate.
